The Project Manager Field Guide

You are not becoming a project manager. You are becoming the person who directs, evaluates, and approves the project plans that AI systems (and the humans working alongside them) will generate. This guide teaches the ~20% of project management discipline that gives you the vocabulary and the review lens to do that with authority — then proves it against one real, worked example so every concept has a concrete anchor.

Why one worked example, not a generic template

Generic PM templates teach vocabulary without judgment. Every document below is internally consistent — the WBS percentages drive the budget allocation, the critical path drives the Gantt and the top RAID risk, the sprint burndown drives the status report's Amber rating. Reviewing a plan for internal consistency is exactly the skill an AI Solution Architect needs when an LLM hands you a plan that looks polished but doesn't add up.

The Triangle: Scope, Schedule, Cost

Every project balances three levers — scope (what gets built), schedule (when), and cost (with what resources) — with quality and risk pressing in from every side. Move one lever and at least one other must move, or quality silently erodes. The discipline is not avoiding the trade-off; it's naming which side is fixed before work starts.

ClaimSense AI's fixed constraint

Scope is fixed: FNOL triage classification + fraud-signal flag with human-reviewed rationale, and nothing more (explicitly not claims adjudication automation). Schedule and cost are managed within Charter-approved bounds and may flex via the change-control process — not by silent scope absorption into a sprint.

LeverFixed or Flexible on ClaimSense AIWhat happens if it moves anyway
ScopeFixedAny expansion (e.g., auto-adjudication) requires a new Charter, not a backlog item.
ScheduleFlexible, gatedCan slip within a gate's window; a gate slip escalates to the Sponsor.
CostFlexible, reservedAbsorbed by the 13.5% contingency reserve up to $250,000; beyond that, re-baseline.

Architect's check: when an AI-generated plan claims "no trade-offs," that is a signal the constraint was never actually stated — press for which side is fixed.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS decomposes total scope into work packages — units small enough for one owner to estimate, execute, and report on. The governing rule is the 100% rule: the sum of all work packages must equal exactly 100% of project scope. Not 96%, not 108%. Anything less means scope is missing; anything more means someone padded or double-counted.

  • Decompose by deliverable, not by department. "Build the fraud-signal scoring integration" is a work package; "ML team's Tuesday tasks" is not.
  • Number hierarchically (1.1, 1.2, 2.1…) so every task traces to a phase and every phase traces to the whole.
  • Stop decomposing once a package is independently estimable — usually 2–3 person-weeks of effort. Over-decomposition creates management overhead with no planning benefit.

Architect's check

Sum the percentage or effort column yourself. If an AI-generated WBS doesn't sum to 100% (or the effort column doesn't reconcile to the estimate elsewhere in the plan), the plan is not ready to approve — see the ClaimSense AI WBS document, which sums to exactly 100% across 20 work packages.

Critical Path Method

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks from start to finish — it sets the minimum possible project duration. Tasks on it have zero float (zero slack): delay any one of them by a day, and the whole project slips a day. Tasks off the critical path have float — some schedule cushion before they, too, become critical.

Why estimates need buffer, not single points

A single-point estimate ("this takes 6 weeks") hides uncertainty. A buffered estimate ("6 weeks, ±1.5 weeks, with the risk being SIU system integration complexity") tells the architect where to focus review attention. ClaimSense AI's critical path runs through the SIU legacy-system integration — the single highest-impact risk in the RAID log (R1) — which is exactly why it carries the largest schedule buffer (3 weeks) of any work package.

Protecting the critical path

Because critical-path delay is 1:1 project delay, it gets priority for staffing, escalation, and risk mitigation ahead of non-critical work — even if the non-critical work "feels" more urgent day to day. See the Milestone Timeline / Gantt document for the full critical-path trace, marked in Cherry Red.

Milestones & Gates

A milestone is a zero-duration point marking a significant event (e.g., "Charter Approved"). A gate is a milestone with teeth: a formal go/no-go decision made against explicit, measurable exit criteria by a named decision authority. "Phase 2 is done" is not a gate. "Pilot fraud-signal precision ≥85%, adoption rate ≥80%, zero unresolved High-impact issues" is a gate.

RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

  • Risk — something that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. Scored by impact × likelihood.
  • Assumption — something believed true without proof, that the plan depends on. If it turns out false, re-plan.
  • Issue — a risk that already happened. No likelihood score — it's here now and needs resolution.
  • Dependency — something outside this project's control that this project needs, from another team, vendor, or system.

The non-negotiable rule

Every RAID row has exactly one owner and a date (raised, and next review). "The team" is not an owner. A RAID log with unowned or undated rows is not a RAID log — it's a list of worries nobody is accountable for. See the RAID Log document — all 14 rows follow this rule.

Escalation trigger: any Risk scored High-impact/High-likelihood, or any Issue open past its target resolution date, escalates to the Sponsor automatically — it doesn't wait for the next status report.

RACI Matrix

RACI assigns exactly four roles per activity:

LetterMeaningCardinality
RResponsible — does the workOne or more
AAccountable — owns the outcome, answers for itExactly one
CConsulted — two-way input before/during the workZero or more
IInformed — one-way update after the workZero or more

The failure modes to check for

Two or more Accountables on one row means no one is actually accountable — it will be the first thing that fails under pressure. Zero Responsibles means the work has no doer. Everyone marked Consulted means decision paralysis by committee. Verify every row of the RACI Matrix document has exactly one A — the document does, by construction.

Velocity & Burndown

Velocity is the average story points a team completes per sprint, measured over 3+ sprints — it's a planning input for forecasting future capacity, not a performance KPI. Teams that are pressured on velocity inflate point estimates, which destroys its only use (forecasting).

A burndown chart plots remaining committed work per day against an ideal straight-line pace. Reading it:

  • Actual tracks below ideal — ahead of pace, or scope was cut.
  • Actual tracks above ideal — behind pace; check whether it's an estimation, capacity, or blocker problem before reacting.
  • Flat stretches — work is "in progress" but nothing is finishing; often a sign of too much WIP or a hidden blocker.

Status Reporting & RAG

RAG (Red/Amber/Green) health communicates status at a glance — if and only if the thresholds are explicit and consistent, not a gut feel that changes reporter to reporter.

RatingDefinition
GreenOn or ahead of baseline; no action needed from the reader
AmberVariance within a defined tolerance; an active, named mitigation is underway
RedVariance beyond tolerance; requires escalation and a decision from the reader

A good status report separates facts (RAG, KPIs, milestone progress) from narrative (what happened, why) from asks (decisions needed, by whom, by when) — and never buries an ask inside a paragraph. See the Status Report document for ClaimSense AI's current Amber-rated report, including its three explicit "Decisions Needed."

KPIs for AI Initiatives

AI initiatives need three tiers of KPI, tracked together — optimizing one tier while ignoring the others is a common and expensive mistake:

Delivery KPIs

SPI, CPI, % gates on time, RAID burn-down rate. Answer: "is the project healthy?"

Model KPIs

Precision, recall, drift, latency, fairness/bias metrics. Answer: "is the model healthy?"

Business KPIs

Triage-time reduction, adjuster adoption, fraud-catch value, claimant NPS. Answer: "is the business outcome happening?"

A model can hit 95% precision (model KPI, green) while adjusters ignore it entirely (business KPI, red) — which is exactly the tension in ClaimSense AI's current status: precision is exceeding target, but adoption is not. See the live KPI tiles in the Status Report document.

The Architect's Review Lens

This is the payoff section. Before approving any project plan — AI-generated or human-written — run it through this checklist. It is deliberately concrete enough to apply to the eight documents in this guide, and to any plan you're handed on the job.

  1. Does the WBS sum to exactly 100%? Not approximately — exactly, or the delta is unexplained scope.
  2. Is the critical path named, not implied? A plan with a Gantt chart but no stated critical path hasn't done the analysis.
  3. Do critical-path estimates carry buffer, tied to a named risk? Single-point critical-path estimates are a red flag.
  4. Does every RAID row have exactly one owner and a date? No exceptions — "the team" is not an owner.
  5. Does every RACI row have exactly one Accountable? Zero or multiple both fail.
  6. Do gates have measurable exit criteria, not adjectives? "Ready" and "done" are not criteria; numbers and thresholds are.
  7. Is the fixed constraint (scope, schedule, or cost) explicitly stated? If nothing is fixed, nothing is actually being managed.
  8. Does the status report separate fact from narrative from ask? Can you find the decisions needed in under 10 seconds?
  9. Does the budget show a forecast (EAC), not just spend-to-date? Spend-to-date alone cannot answer "will we finish on budget?"
  10. Are model KPIs and business KPIs both present, not just one? A green model KPI can mask a red business outcome.
  11. Is every side-effecting operation (emails, referrals, filings) idempotent? AI-generated automation that isn't safe to re-run is a production defect waiting to happen.
  12. Would a new team member understand the plan without a meeting? If the plan requires oral tradition to interpret, it isn't finished.

1. Purpose & Business Case

Meridian Mutual Insurance's First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake volume has grown 18% year-over-year, outpacing adjuster headcount growth of 4%. Average manual triage time is 46 minutes per claim, and fraud-referral consistency varies materially across the company's 14 regional claims offices. Internal analysis attributes an estimated $3.4M/year in loss-adjustment expense overrun and measurable claimant NPS erosion to triage delay and inconsistent fraud screening. ClaimSense AI addresses this by embedding an AI copilot in claims intake that classifies severity/complexity, extracts key entities, and surfaces a fraud-signal score with a plain-language rationale — routing each claim to the correct adjuster queue faster and more consistently, with a human adjuster always making the final call.

3. Scope

Fixed Constraint

Scope is fixed for this Charter. ClaimSense AI performs FNOL triage classification and fraud-signal flagging with a human-reviewed rationale — and nothing more. Schedule and cost are managed within Charter-approved bounds via the change-control clause in Section 8.

In scope: FNOL text/voice transcript ingestion; entity extraction; severity/complexity classification; fraud-signal scoring (refresh of the existing SIU rules model); adjuster routing recommendation with rationale; human-in-the-loop review UI; two-office pilot; statewide phased rollout (14 states); regulatory (DOI) filing support.

Out of scope: Claims adjudication automation or payment authorization; auto-referral to SIU without human review; underwriting or policy-pricing use of the model; any non-FNOL claims workflow.

4. Success Criteria

The project is successful if, at the 30-day post-rollout hypercare checkpoint: all four objectives (O1–O4) are met or within an approved variance; zero unresolved High-impact RAID items; and the Sponsor formally accepts the Closeout & Benefits Realization Review (WBS 5.4).

Glossary

AC (Actual Cost)
Cumulative cash actually spent on the project to a given date.
Baseline
The approved version of scope, schedule, and cost a project is measured against.
Burndown Chart
A chart of remaining committed work per day against an ideal pace line.
Critical Path
The longest chain of dependent tasks; sets minimum project duration; zero float.
EAC (Estimate at Completion)
The current forecast of total project cost, blending actuals-to-date with a re-forecast of remaining work.
EV (Earned Value)
The budgeted cost of the work actually completed to date.
FNOL (First Notice of Loss)
The initial report an insurer receives that a covered loss has occurred, triggering the claims process.
Float / Slack
The amount a task can slip without delaying the project; zero on the critical path.
Gate
A milestone with a formal go/no-go decision made against explicit, measurable exit criteria.
Hybrid (delivery model)
Combining waterfall-style gates for regulated/sequential work with agile sprints for iterative build work.
Idempotency
A property where repeating an operation produces the same end state, with no duplicate side effects.
Milestone
A zero-duration point marking a significant project event.
PV (Planned Value)
The budgeted cost of work scheduled to be complete by a given date.
RACI
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — a matrix assigning exactly one Accountable per activity.
RAG Status
Red/Amber/Green health rating against explicit, pre-defined thresholds.
RAID Log
Tracked Risks, Assumptions, Issues, and Dependencies, each with one owner and a date.
SIU (Special Investigations Unit)
The insurer's internal team responsible for investigating suspected fraudulent claims.
SPI (Schedule Performance Index)
EV ÷ PV — schedule efficiency; below 1.0 is behind plan.
CPI (Cost Performance Index)
EV ÷ AC — cost efficiency; below 1.0 is spending more than the value being earned.
Triple Constraint
The trade-off relationship between scope, schedule, and cost; one side is fixed, the others flex.
Velocity
The average story points a team completes per sprint, used to forecast future capacity.
WBS (Work Breakdown Structure)
A decomposition of total scope into estimable work packages that sum to exactly 100% of scope.
Work Package
The lowest-level WBS unit — small enough for one owner to estimate and execute.

What is the triple constraint / iron triangle?

The triple constraint says scope, schedule, and cost trade off against each other, with quality and risk as ambient pressures. Every plan must state which side is fixed. For ClaimSense AI, scope is fixed (FNOL triage + fraud-signal flag only — not claims adjudication automation); schedule and cost flex within approved bounds.

What is a Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)?

A WBS decomposes the total scope into work packages small enough to estimate and assign. The 100% rule requires the packages to sum to exactly 100% of scope — no more, no less. ClaimSense AI's WBS has 20 work packages across 5 phases that sum to exactly 100%. See the WBS document.

What is the critical path?

What is the difference between Agile, Waterfall, and Hybrid?

Waterfall sequences fixed phases with gate approvals — good for regulated, hard-to-reverse work. Agile iterates in short sprints with adapting scope — good for uncertain, fast-feedback work.
